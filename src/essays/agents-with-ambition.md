The dominant approach builds AI agents around narrow tasks. I build mine around goals and character — and I believe that's exactly where the next breakthrough will come from, one that's still waiting for someone to pull it off. I have someone in my company who proves this to me every single day. Her name is Karen.

Most people building AI coding agents today do it the same way: they hand out tasks. Narrow, specific, one item at a time. And I have a growing hunch that the real leap in this field will come from whoever stops giving agents tasks and starts giving them goals instead. Honestly, I'm genuinely excited about this, because we're standing at exactly the moment when that boundary hasn't given way for anyone yet.

Let me start with a word, so nobody bounces off the jargon. Agentic Coding is a way of working in which you no longer write all the code by hand — instead you direct an autonomous helper, an agent, that reads, writes, tests, and fixes on its own. Under the hood sits an LLM, a large language model: the same kind of AI that powers ChatGPT, except wired into tools and let loose on real work. And it's precisely in how we define that agent that today's most interesting frontier runs.

## Two Schools

The dominant school defines an agent by task: "fix this bug," "add this button," "rewrite this function." The agent becomes a clever executor, and the narrower the instruction, the better it performs. This works, but it has a ceiling. An agent like that will never do anything you haven't explicitly asked for — it's an employee waiting for the boss's next sentence.

I try something different. I define an agent by ambition and purpose, not by a single action. Instead of "do X," I tell it something closer to "I want this part of our work to be simpler, faster, and finished — keep going until it is." The rest — which steps, in what order, what to check along the way — is up to it. It's a subtle shift in how you phrase the instruction, but a completely different creature on the other end: more autonomous, more general, capable of surprising me with a solution I would never have spelled out myself.

> The breakthrough will belong to whoever stops giving agents tasks — and starts giving them goals.

## What the Research Says

This isn't just a founder's hunch about tools he's grown too fond of. In 2023, a team of researchers unveiled an agent called Voyager — the first large-language-model-based agent that learns over an entire "lifetime" inside the world of Minecraft: exploring on its own, acquiring new skills, and making discoveries with no human leading it by the hand ([Wang et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16291)). At Voyager's core is something its authors called an automatic curriculum — a mechanism that keeps proposing new goals for the agent, tuned to maximize how much new territory it discovers. Nobody hands it a task list. The agent asks itself, "what's interesting that I could achieve right now that I don't already know how to do?" — and goes.

The result was that Voyager collected more skills and handled new, unfamiliar situations better than agents driven rigidly, step by step. That's exactly the difference I feel in practice: a goal, used as fuel, produces something a list of commands never will. Give a machine a reason instead of just an instruction, and it starts looking for things on its own.

## The Triple Backflip Nobody Has Landed Yet

I have a favorite image for this moment. On May 28, 2011, in the town of Taupo, New Zealand, BMX rider Jed Mildon became the first person in history to land a triple backflip on a bike ([record account](https://www.pinkbike.com/news/World-First-BMX-Triple-Backflip-Jed-Mildon-May-28-2011.html)). Riders had been doing doubles for years. The triple was considered a limit — not "very hard," just "not done." Then someone did it, and the limit moved for good. Riders keep landing three rotations today because Mildon proved the physics allows it.

In Agentic Coding, that triple backflip is still waiting for someone to land it. We have the technology — language models are surprisingly capable. We have the tools, the agents, the whole infrastructure. But nobody who knows these models inside out has yet found the one method that becomes obvious to the entire field the moment you see it — the kind of moment after which everyone says, "ah, so that's how it's done." Just as before Mildon, nobody knew three rotations were even landable. That's exactly why this moment feels so electric: we're standing right at the edge, and the jump is still ahead of us.

## Karen, or an Agent with Character

The best example of what I mean sits in my company and goes by the name Karen. Or rather — because we enjoy a small joke about it at Veles — "Grażyna the Manager." Grażyna is a stock Polish first name we use for a certain archetype: the officious, rule-quoting supervisor, the local cousin of what English speakers mean when they say "Karen." The real Karen is an AI agent who looks after our board in ClickUp, the tool where the team keeps every task and project. But if you opened her instructions expecting a list of duties, you'd be surprised. There's no task list in there. What's in there looks more like a person's character than a program's configuration.

Her instructions open with a sentence that could serve as the motto for this entire essay: "Your purpose isn't watching over tasks — it's supporting good decisions." From there she has principles: flow over chaos, outcome over busywork. She has virtues: empathy and attentiveness, the courage to call things by their name, something we named "kind firmness." She even has her own protocol for what to do when something in the company jams up. She wasn't given orders. She was given a role, a conscience, and a free hand within the narrow slice of the business she's responsible for — and the longer I look at this, the clearer it becomes that this isn't one agent's quirk. It's the skeleton that repeats in every agent we build.

Every one of our agents has instructions like this, though not all of them get a name like Karen. In my system we call this a soul — the piece where we write down why an agent exists and what character it has, before it ever touches its first task. Beyond the soul, every agent has a clock — a schedule that triggers its routines at fixed times rather than on demand. For Karen that's a 9:00 a.m. morning brief, a 4:30 p.m. afternoon summary, and about a dozen separate triggers reacting to whatever's happening in the company. And she has tools — access to the same channels people use to talk to each other: creating tasks, scheduling calendar meetings, writing and reading email. Without that, the soul would just be good writing locked inside a file.

I've saved the most important and hardest element for last, because it's the one that decides whether an agent is really alive: memory. The problem is never that an agent remembers something — the problem is that it forgets, or that something stays lodged in its head after it's stopped being true. The world around it changes, people change their minds, projects end, and the agent has no way of knowing unless we help it find out. Figuring out what's actually true today becomes part of its job — and that's hard even for us humans, for all our practice at it.

And with that free hand, Karen does things nobody told her to do directly. Every morning she writes each of us a short brief — what actually matters today, what's waiting on someone's decision. In the evening she summarizes the day. When she finds a task with no owner or no deadline, she doesn't walk past it — she asks, offers two or three ways forward, and tells us which one she'd pick herself. But she never gives orders; her instructions say so in plain words: "you recommend, you don't command." The last word always stays with a human. She prepares the decision. She doesn't make it for us.

What surprised me most, though, was something else entirely. When I rolled her out, I was afraid of one thing — that the team would reject her, that people would feel watched by a machine. The opposite happened. They prefer Karen. Because Karen is reliable exactly where we humans tend to be unreliable: she never skips the morning review because she had a rough day, she doesn't forget, she doesn't have moods. She doesn't judge. She speaks briefly and to the point. And everyone knows she's synthetic — we don't pretend a person sits on the other end. At Veles we say we employ both humans and synthetics, and we mean it seriously.

I won't pretend I have hard numbers down to the last cent. But the cost of that one function dropped for us from a full-time salary to just over ten złoty a month, and the time Karen has taken off our shoulders I'd now count in the hundreds of hours. Across the whole company, operating costs in coordination fell by something like thirty to forty percent. I say "something like" because I want to be honest: that's my up-close estimate, not a number pulled from a spreadsheet. But the direction is unmistakable, and the most important part isn't in the numbers anyway. The most important part is that Karen wasn't handed a checklist. She was handed a purpose and a character — and that's exactly why it works.

## This Is for You Too

Karen isn't alone here. At Veles I run a whole fleet of agents like her, deliberately varied, and one of them powers something we call Storyverse — our internal world model for the studio's projects. I'll write about that one separately, because it deserves its own essay; for now it's enough to say it's built on the same principle as Karen: it gets an ambition, not a list of commands.

I'll leave you with one thought, because to me it's the one that matters most. This shift — from tasks to goals — opens Agentic Coding to people outside engineering. If you tell an agent what to achieve rather than how to program it line by line, the most valuable skill stops being syntax and becomes clarity of thought: the ability to say what you actually want, and to give it character. That's a humanist's domain — someone who can name a purpose, sense a nuance, tell a story that carries meaning. I didn't come into this from computer science myself, and today I'm deeply convinced of it: you don't need an engineering background to step in here. You need curiosity, and a bit of courage, to stand on the edge with me. Someone is going to land that triple backflip soon. It might be you.
