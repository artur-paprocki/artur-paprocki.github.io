More than two million people in Poland now watch vertical drama series on their phones — not in some niche app, but in ReelShort, where in May 2026 [1.9 million people in Poland](https://screenlovers.pl/mikro-dramy-maxi-ratingi-czyli-o-mikroseriale-w-walce-o-polskiego-widza/) spent an average of five and a half hours a month, a scale confirmed by Poland's internet-audience measurement study, run by the [Polskie Badania Internetu](https://pbi.org.pl/) institute; [Tygodnik Powszechny](https://www.tygodnikpowszechny.pl/short-dramy-mikrodramy-reelshort-wow-krzyzacy-ai) wrote about it too. The quality is embarrassing: the same narrative beats every episode — the secret billionaire, the betrayal, a twist every few seconds so nobody swipes away. Every episode ends on a hook that shoves the viewer into the next one, on and on. WoW, a Polish platform, already has its own original productions for the local market, including "Krzyżacy" ("The Teutonic Knights"), an adaptation generated entirely by AI. In China the machine turns almost without a human hand: DataEye data cited by MIT Technology Review puts output at roughly [470 micro-dramas](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/05/15/1137326/chinese-short-dramas-ai/) a day. An assembly line, not a craft — and yet people watch.

Here's what makes it interesting: this crude format beats streaming exactly where it should hurt most — viewer attention. Omdia found that in the fourth quarter of 2025, [a ReelShort user spent 35.7 minutes a day inside the app, a Netflix user 24.8](https://www.decodetv.com/2026/02/25/omdia-micro-dramas-outperforming-streamers-in-minutes-watched/); on mobile, ReelShort already closes in on half of Netflix's number, despite a far smaller audience. So the format isn't the problem. Nobody has yet done for the vertical rectangle what the masters of cinema did for the horizontal screen: they turned the constraints of frame, cut, and time into a language, and the 9:16 portrait is still waiting for its own. It has no Hitchcock yet. I've written before that craft outlives format — that was only half the thought. The other half: the format itself still has nobody to fit that craft to.

In Vertigo (1958), Hitchcock needed a shot the camera couldn't yet take: looking straight down a bell-tower stairwell so the audience would feel the height and the character's vertigo, not understand it. The fix came from Irmin Roberts, a second-unit cameraman at Paramount: the camera pulls back while the lens zooms in, changing focal length — the figure stays the same size in frame, only the background perspective stretches and warps. It didn't work on the full-size set, so the crew shot the stairwell miniature lying on its side, horizontal instead of vertical. They didn't route around the limit. They turned it into a sentence cinema's grammar didn't have yet. Today it's called the Vertigo effect, or the dolly zoom.

The proof of that vacancy is almost comically literal. Chinese screenwriter Phoenix Zhu told MIT Technology Review that writing for today's AI models, he can no longer put "he gave her a cold stare" into a script — the model can't act it. He has to spell it out — ["Cold beams of light shot out from his eyes"](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/05/15/1137326/chinese-short-dramas-ai/) — or the emotion never reaches the screen. Faces have the same problem — [models lose an actor's identity between shots](https://news.skrew.ai/chinese-short-dramas-ai-content-machines/), so studios that want a viewer back for the next episode go back to real people on set. The hardware is ready. The craft is still missing.

But this pattern isn't showing up here for the first time — a despised format, branded dangerous for young minds, that turns out to carry something bigger anyway. History doesn't repeat itself, it rhymes, and the clearest rhyme sounds in the comic book.

On April 19, 1954, American psychiatrist Fredric Wertham published Seduction of the Innocent, declaring the comic book ["a harmful form of popular literature and a serious cause of juvenile delinquency"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seduction_of_the_Innocent). The result: U.S. Senate hearings that same year, and the Comics Code Authority — self-censorship that gutted the medium of anything beyond polite superhero adventures for decades. It took half a century for Carol Tilley, a researcher at the University of Illinois, to go through Wertham's notes and show he had ["manipulated, overstated, compromised and fabricated evidence"](https://ischool.illinois.edu/news-events/news/2013/02/new-york-times-features-tilleys-comics-research) — changing children's ages, splicing quotes to fit a thesis decided in advance. The moral panic had the trappings of science and none of the substance.

> Contempt for a new medium always arrives first, and it's always wrong — the only question is how many decades it takes before someone checks.

Parallel to that panic, the comic book was quietly doing the last thing anyone expected of it: teaching people to read. The Swiss schoolteacher Rodolphe Töpffer began drawing his picture stories back in the 1820s. He drew the first of them, "Histoire de Mr. Vieux Bois," in 1827, and published it in print ten years later. He called them ["littérature en estampes"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodolphe_T%C3%B6pffer) — literature in prints — and wrote that they had [found their way to a wide audience](https://imagetextjournal.com/the-rhetorical-invention-of-comics-a-selection-of-rodolphe-topffers-late-reflections-on-composing-image-text-narratives/), to people who would never have picked up a sermon or a serious book. A hundred and fifty years later, researchers Ujiie and Krashen studied a group of seventh-grade boys and reached a conclusion simpler than anyone expected: [children who read more comics also read more books, and got more pleasure out of reading in general](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ527305). The picture doesn't pull you away from the word. It leads you to it.

The comic book also had its moment in the war for the mass reader. In 1895, Richard F. Outcault drew [the Yellow Kid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Yellow_Kid) for Pulitzer's paper; a year later, Pulitzer and Hearst fought over the character — a circulation war that gave us the term "yellow journalism." A picture story drove the era's most mass medium. Nobody praised it for that. It worked anyway.

The comic book shows a despised format can carry literacy. There's another thread here — not just what people read, but where. Before the micro-drama took the viewer into the subway, Netflix took them out of the cinema and into the living room: the service launched streaming in 2007, and six years later released [House of Cards, an entire season dropped at once](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netflix) — coining binge-watching. But someone else had ruled that room for decades. The "soap opera" took its name literally from its sponsors — [radio dramas from the 1930s were financed by soap manufacturers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soap_opera), aired in the daytime slot [for housewives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soap_opera). The first was Irna Phillips's Painted Dreams — a fifteen-minute daily family drama. That room has been ruled by the woman of the house for a hundred years — I say it without a trace of contempt, since I work the same emotional register today, just in a different vessel.

The micro-drama takes the viewer further: out of the living room, onto a phone held upright on the subway. And again, just elsewhere. In Japan, manga is commuter reading: [during rush hour, subway cars are full of salarymen reading](https://factsanddetails.com/Japan/cat20/sub135/item739.html). At its peak, [Weekly Shōnen Jump sold 6.53 million copies weekly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weekly_Sh%C5%8Dnen_Jump). In the 2000s, the same thing happened on the phone: keitai shōsetsu, novels written and read on a mobile, took off so hard that in 2007, [five of Japan's ten best-selling novels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_phone_novel) had started life there. Nobody spoke of it with pride — people just read, because they had the text in hand when they had the time.

Europe is just getting its first taste. Spain's Atresmedia released a vertical micro-drama on Atresplayer, "Una Novia por Navidad" — [more than twenty million plays and a commissioned sequel](https://todotvnews.com/atresplayer-estrena-la-segunda-temporada-de-su-microdrama-vertical-una-novia-por-vacaciones/). Too early to call a direction, but worth asking already.

> Cinema had its authors. The living room had the soap opera. The subway is waiting for someone who understands that the picture leads to the word, instead of pulling you away from it.

That it can be done differently — sensuously, wisely — is shown by ["The Butterfly Effect"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JQa1437ckCA), Anne Horel's five-minute film, produced by Partizan, France, 2026. She says outright she built it end-to-end in AI, yet not one glossy plastic face appears on screen, only gouache, line, painterly texture. The look comes from her own models, trained on her analog collages and on public-domain archives: Maria Sibylla Merian's 1705 botanical plates, John Gould's natural-history illustrations — a European bestiary and herbarium, the European comic book's lineage. Le Lombard, the house that launched Tintin magazine, rejects AI in comics outright, on the grounds that such art is generated through theft from artists; Horel answers with craft, training on herself and on what is public. Even the sound she recorded with her own mouth, then ran through a processor. The frame that stays with me: a black cat curled up with a white kitten among garlic bulbs and painted raindrops.

Veles, the company I run, produces micro-dramas today — with real actors, not a pure AI assembly line, and I say that openly, it's no secret. Veles Productions, with a Story Room at its disposal and using XR and AI technology, can produce both illustrated animated comics and dramas. I see up close how low the bar can be, and how much viewers keep coming back anyway. Two facts at once, not one contradicting the other.

The lineage that interests me most in all this isn't cinema, and it isn't the soap opera. It's the comic book — a picture story that reached people wherever they happened to be, and led them back to reading instead of pulling them away from it. Watching a European market just taking its first steps in this format, the question I keep asking is what the micro-drama becomes here. Another soap opera turned vertical, a telenovela copied from the living room into your pocket? Or a renaissance of the animated comic strip — a picture story for someone with twenty minutes on the subway and nothing else. I don't know the answer. Today the European vertical is claimed almost entirely by photorealistic romance and thriller, and the drawing hand — the same one the European comic book grew out of — I haven't yet seen in it; maybe because these two worlds simply haven't met yet. But maybe Veles Productions will turn out to be the Hitchcock of the vertical format — not because it already is one, only because someone, eventually, has to be the first to try. Someone has to invent, for the vertical frame, the shot that doesn't exist yet — the way someone had to invent that one, in 1958, in a bell-tower stairwell. If you're searching for a grammar in this format yourself, not just a hook for the next episode, I have a feeling we'd have something to talk about.
